When Prediction Doesn’t Pay: Recency Is Near-Optimal for LLM Prefix Caches

Sanjeev Ganjihal

Abstract

Prefix caching lets an LLM serving engine reuse the key–value (KV) state of a shared prompt prefix instead of recomputing it, and a growing body of work proposes learned or statistical predictors to decide which KV blocks to keep resident. In earlier work we ourselves proposed a Bayesian reuse predictor and projected large gains from predictive eviction [2]. This paper measures whether such prediction can pay. We replay real public LLM workloads, a multi-turn conversational corpus (OpenAssistant/oasst1) and a coding-agent corpus (CodeAct), through a prefix-block cache simulator that computes the clairvoyant Belady optimum (opt) alongside FIFO, LRU, LFU, SIEVE, and S3-FIFO. Two findings follow. First, on both workloads the recency family is already at or within 0.9 points of the opt hit ratio at realistic capacity pressure (LRU reaches 99.4 to 100 percent of opt), while frequency-based eviction collapses (LFU falls to 40 to 79 percent of opt). Second, because no policy can exceed opt, the measured opt-minus-LRU gap upper-bounds the benefit of any reuse predictor, statistical or learned: on these traces that headroom is at most 0.5 points, and often zero. Our earlier projection therefore does not survive measurement, and we say so: a Beta-conjugate reuse predictor cannot recover more than the headroom that recency already captures. We close the loop on real hardware: replaying the same traces against a production engine (vLLM 0.27.1, Qwen2.5-32B, tensor-parallel 8, two 8× NVIDIA H100 nodes), the engine’s own prefix-cache counters report 65.3 and 73.1 percent achieved hit ratios, 99.8 to 102.8 percent of the simulated opt ceiling, so a deployed recency-managed cache already banks the optimum that no predictor can exceed; re-serving the same trace with the cache pinned to the simulator’s capacity, the engine also reproduces the frontier live (73.1 percent hit ratio sequential vs. 47.0 percent under 256-way interleave, against simulated LRU’s 73.0 and 50.2). Running our own Beta predictor as the eviction policy confirms the bound the hard way: it reaches only 75 to 91 percent of opt against LRU’s 99.4 to 100, converting prediction error into eviction error. We then measure the one exception, which is in kind rather than degree: structural signals (session continuation across tool-call pauses) beat recency exactly where a dimensionless pause sweep says they should. Interleaving C concurrent sessions, recency holds 99+ percent of opt until the inter-turn gap crosses the cache’s recency horizon, then collapses (71.8 percent of opt on agent traces, 8.2 percent on chat at C=256) while a structural session-hold retains 91.9 to 97.4 percent (65.6 on chat); the statistical predictor overtakes LRU only in this post-collapse regime and never approaches the structural hold. Finally, we show these results do not transfer from the object-cache world where SIEVE and S3-FIFO were validated, and calibrate: the same binaries reproduce the published ordering (SIEVE > S3-FIFO > LRU > FIFO > LFU) on a public 1M-access Twitter-cache trace, then invert it on prefix-block streams, so the disagreement is workload, not implementation.

AI Overview

The Computational Cost of LLM Prefill and the Role of Caching

Large language model (LLM) serving is a resource-intensive process, largely divided into two phases: prefill and decoding. In the prefill phase, the model processes the initial input prompt to generate the key-value (KV) states required for attention mechanisms. The decoding phase then uses these states to generate new tokens one by one. Recent production data suggests that the computational load is heavily skewed toward prefill, with some workloads exhibiting a ratio of 14 input tokens processed for every single output token generated.

To address this bottleneck, system designers utilize "prefix caching." This technique allows an LLM serving engine to store the KV states of common prompt prefixes—such as system instructions, few-shot examples, or the history of a multi-turn conversation—in memory. When a new request arrives that shares a prefix with a previously processed prompt, the engine can simply retrieve the stored KV blocks instead of recomputing them.

Figure 1: A comparison of various cache eviction policies showing their performance relative to the theoretical Belady optimum (OPT). Recency-based policies like LRU and FIFO perform near the optimum for typical LLM workloads.

A critical challenge in managing these caches is the "eviction policy"—the logic that decides which data to discard when the cache reaches its memory limit. Traditional systems intuition, often applied to web caches or database systems, suggests that predictive or statistical models should be used to guess which data is most likely to be reused. This research investigates whether such complex prediction mechanisms actually provide a benefit in the specific context of LLM serving, or if simpler rules are sufficient.

The Mechanics of Prefix Block Caching

In a prefix cache, prompts are not stored as single units. Instead, they are broken down into discrete blocks. For a prompt containing TTT tokens and a system using a block size of BBB tokens, the prompt is represented as a chain of ⌈T/B⌉\lceil T/B \rceil⌈T/B⌉ blocks. Each block is identified by a unique key, typically a hash of the entire sequence of tokens preceding it, including the tokens within the block itself.

This structure creates a specific access pattern. In multi-turn conversations or agentic workflows, prompts grow sequentially. For example, a three-turn conversation might look like this:

  1. User: "Hello" →\to→ Block A
  2. User: "Hello, how are you?" →\to→ Block A + Block B
  3. User: "Hello, how are you? Tell me a joke." →\to→ Block A + Block B + Block C

Every time the user adds to the conversation, the system re-accesses the earlier blocks (A and B) before processing the new one (C). Because of this "chained" nature, reuse is highly predictable: if you are in the middle of a session, you are almost certain to need the immediate prefix blocks again very soon.

The research evaluated these patterns using two primary workloads: a multi-turn chat workload (OpenAssistant) and a coding agent workload (CodeAct). These traces preserve the chronological growth of prompts, providing a realistic look at how memory pressure affects cache performance.

Evaluating Eviction Policies: Simple vs. Predictive

The study compared several classes of eviction policies to determine their effectiveness:

  1. Standard Zero-Knowledge Policies: These include LRU (Least Recently Used), which discards the block not accessed for the longest time; FIFO (First-In, First-Out), which discards the oldest block; and LFU (Least Frequently Used), which keeps blocks with the highest total hit counts.
  2. Advanced Object Cache Policies: Policies like SIEVE and S3-FIFO, which are designed to outperform LRU in general computing environments by distinguishing between "one-hit wonders" and frequently accessed items.
  3. Statistical Predictors: The research tested a Beta Reuse Predictor, which uses a Bayesian approach to calculate the probability that a block will be reused based on past evidence. It assigns a Beta(1,1)\text{Beta}(1,1)Beta(1,1) prior to each block category and updates the posterior mean as hits or evictions occur.
  4. The Belady Optimum (OPT): This is a theoretical, "clairvoyant" algorithm that knows the future. It always evicts the block that will be needed furthest in the future. While impossible to implement in a real-time system, it serves as the absolute ceiling for cache performance.

To ensure the results were not just theoretical artifacts, the researchers validated their simulations on production hardware using a vLLM serving engine powered by 16 NVIDIA H100 GPUs. They used a block model where W=12W = 12W=12 words per block, approximating standard 16-token configurations.

The Vanishing Headroom for Prediction

The most striking finding of the research is that for standard LLM workloads, simple recency-based policies like LRU are nearly perfect. In simulations where requests were processed sequentially (representing typical user-model interaction), LRU achieved hit ratios between 99.4% and 100% of the theoretical Belady optimum.

Figure 2: This chart illustrates "rank inversion." While advanced policies like SIEVE outperform LRU on standard object traces (like Twitter's), they actually perform worse on LLM prefix traces, where LRU is near-optimal.

This discovery reveals that the "headroom"—the potential gain from moving from LRU to a more sophisticated policy—is virtually non-existent. Specifically, the gap Hopt(c)−HLRU(c)H_{\text{opt}}(c) - H_{\text{LRU}}(c)Hopt​(c)−HLRU​(c) was found to be at most 0.5 points. Because LRU is already so close to the ceiling, even the most perfect machine learning model could only improve performance by a negligible fraction.

Interestingly, the study found a "rank inversion" when comparing LLM caches to traditional object caches (like those used by Twitter). While advanced policies like SIEVE are superior for general web traffic, they perform worse than LRU on LLM prefix streams. Furthermore, the statistical Beta predictor actually performed worse than LRU in most cases, as its prediction errors led to "eviction errors"—discarding blocks that were about to be reused.

The Pause Frontier: Where Simple Rules Fail

While recency is optimal for sequential interactions, the researchers identified a specific scenario where it collapses: the "pause frontier." This occurs when there is high concurrency (CCC concurrent sessions) or significant "think time" between turns, causing the time between re-accessing a prefix block to exceed the cache's "recency horizon."

The recency horizon is effectively the cache capacity divided by the number of new blocks generated per turn. If a session remains idle for too long while other sessions fill the cache, LRU will evict that session's prefix blocks just before they are needed again.

Figure 3: As concurrency CCC increases, the time between turns for a single session grows. Once this "pause" crosses the recency horizon, LRU's performance drops sharply.

In these high-pressure scenarios (e.g., C=256C=256C=256), LRU's performance can drop to as low as 8.2% of the optimum. However, even here, statistical prediction is not the best answer. Instead, the research suggests using structural signals. A simple "Session-Hold" policy—which prevents the eviction of blocks belonging to active, albeit paused, sessions—outperformed both LRU and the statistical Beta predictor by a wide margin.

Practical Implications for LLM Infrastructure

The findings of this paper offer several concrete takeaways for developers of LLM serving frameworks and AI infrastructure:

  • Avoid Over-Engineering: There is little value in developing complex, learned, or statistical eviction policies for general LLM prefix caching. The inherent structure of the workload (sequential growth and chained re-access) makes simple recency-based management (LRU/FIFO) highly efficient.
  • Focus on Session Awareness: Rather than trying to predict reuse probabilities mathematically, systems should focus on deterministic structural signals. Knowing that a session is "active" but currently waiting for a tool response or user input is a much more powerful signal for cache retention than any statistical model.
  • Capacity Planning: The "pause frontier" provides a mathematical tool for capacity planning. If a system knows its average session length and expected concurrency, it can calculate the minimum cache size required to prevent the "recency collapse."
  • Validation Matters: The research highlights that cache wisdom from other domains (like web caching) does not necessarily translate to LLMs. The unique sequential nature of LLM attention data requires domain-specific measurement.

By demonstrating that "prediction doesn't pay," the research advocates for simpler, more robust system designs that leverage the natural behavior of LLM workloads rather than attempting to model them through complex heuristics.
