 Abstract 
 The emergence of large reasoning models demonstrates that scaling inference-time compute significantly enhances performance on complex tasks.
However, it often falls into another trap: overthinking simple problems, where repetitive rationales yield minimal accuracy gains at a disproportionately high cost.
This motivates adaptive reasoning : dynamically aligning reasoning depth with instance difficulty.
In this paper, we study adaptive reasoning from an optimality perspective, formalizing it as a utility maximization problem where tokens are allocated until the marginal accuracy gain falls below the incremental cost.
Based on this, we propose Coda ( Co mpute Allocation by D ifficulty A wareness), a method that operationalizes this principle by allocating tokens via a policy-internal difficulty signal.
Specifically, Coda estimates difficulty via group-based rollouts and maps it to two non-negative gates that modulate a length-dependent shaping term on top of the binary base reward.
The easy-side gate penalizes verbosity on simple instances, whereas the hard-side gate encourages more deliberative rollouts on challenging ones.
Across model scales and benchmarks, Coda achieves adaptive reasoning without external annotations or user-provided budgets:
on easy tasks, Coda reduces token costs by over 60 % 60\% while maintaining strong accuracy, whereas on hard tasks it incentivizes more deliberative rollouts to maximize performance.
 1 Introduction
 Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) highlights the value of test-time scaling for reasoning [ 16 ; 24 ; 14 ; 8 ] .
Specifically, Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has facilitated the emergence of large reasoning models (LRMs) by leveraging simple rule-based rewards through algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 27 ] , effectively eliciting Long Chain-of-Thought (Long CoT) reasoning [ 40 ; 13 ] .
However, longer reasoning also increases inference cost and can induce overthinking.
On many easy instances, models tend to produce verbose narratives that include redundant steps with little or no gain in correctness [ 34 ; 30 ; 5 ] .
This mismatch between compute and utility creates a practical bottleneck for deploying reasoning models at scale [ 15 ] .
 A natural idea is to penalize length during training, which can reduce token usage but often hurts accuracy on hard instances requiring deep reasoning [ 36 ; 37 ; 3 ] .
Alternatively, methods like L1 [ 1 ] allow users to specify a target budget during inference.
As Figure 1(b) shows, this provides direct control but is often sensitive to difficulty: underestimating the budget can hurt performance, while overestimating it leads to wasted compute [ 12 ; 35 ] .
Broadly, many approaches described as adaptive reasoning trade compute for accuracy by deciding when to think more, yet their efficiency gains can still come from accepting modest performance drops for cost savings over typical inputs [ 18 ; 33 ; 41 ] .
Together, these limitations motivate correctness-aligned objectives that allocate compute by difficulty.

 In this work, we study adaptive compute allocation from an optimality perspective.
Viewing generation length as a controllable budget, the optimal policy should spend tokens until the marginal accuracy gain falls below its cost.
Crucially, this balance point depends on difficulty: as shown in Figure 1(b) , easy tasks saturate quickly so extra tokens yield marginal gains, whereas hard tasks continue to improve with more compute.
This suggests that adaptive reasoning should allocate compute by instance difficulty, rather than uniformly shortening or lengthening generations.
 Motivated by this principle, we propose Coda ( Co mpute Allocation by D ifficulty A wareness), which operationalizes difficulty-aware token pricing using a policy-dependent signal.
Specifically, Coda estimates difficulty via the group success rate s q s_{q} : higher s q s_{q} indicates easier instances for the current policy, and lower s q s_{q} indicates harder ones.
This signal is mapped to two non-negative gates, w q easy w_{q}^{\text{easy}} and w q hard w_{q}^{\text{hard}} , which scale a length-dependent shaping term on the binary base reward.
Specifically, w q easy w_{q}^{\text{easy}} increases the penalty for lengthy outputs when an instance appears easy, discouraging unnecessary verbosity, whereas w q hard w_{q}^{\text{hard}} adds a bonus for more deliberative rollouts when an instance appears hard, encouraging deeper reasoning when it is likely to help.
As shown in Figure 1 , Coda learns an adaptive allocation rule, consuming substantially fewer tokens on easier problems while reserving more compute for harder ones, without requiring external annotations or user-specified budgets.
 Our results and analysis further reveal that:
 1) Coda is robust to difficulty shifts.
Under extreme easy- or hard-skewed training, the gating dynamics adjust accordingly while preserving the same qualitative behavior: avoiding unhelpful reasoning and allowing deeper reasoning when needed.
 2) The easy-side penalty acts as a selective regularizer rather than a blunt truncation.
A moderate penalty trims redundancy without sacrificing base reward, whereas an excessive penalty suppresses exploration.
The hard-side bonus must be correctness-aligned, and rewarding length on incorrect outputs induces degenerate length-seeking without improving accuracy.
 3) Coda changes reasoning behavior beyond output length control.
On easy questions, it mitigates overthinking by reducing redundant restatement.
On hard questions, it preserves reflective Long CoT, matching GRPO’s reflection patterns while avoiding the indiscriminate truncation of length-penalty baselines.
 In summary, we highlight our contributions:
 1) An optimality formulation for difficulty-aware compute allocation. We formulate compute allocation as utility maximization under token costs, showing that difficulty induces different effective token prices and motivating difficulty-conditioned cost weighting of tokens.
 2) Dual-gated length shaping from rollout-based difficulty. We introduce Coda , which estimates difficulty via group success rate s q s_{q} and maps it to w q easy w_{q}^{\text{easy}} and w q hard w_{q}^{\text{hard}} to modulate a length-dependent shaping term, penalizing verbosity on easy instances and providing a bonus for deliberative, correctness-aligned rollouts on hard ones.
 3) Evidence for genuine, robust adaptiveness. Coda exhibits adaptive reasoning: it allocates tokens by difficulty, remains stable under extreme skews, and is supported by ablations and reasoning-pattern analyses.
 2 Optimality View of Adaptive Compute
 Compute as a controllable resource.
 For autoregressive language models, generation length provides a practical proxy for measuring inference compute.
Let n ⁡ ( o ) n(o) denote the number of generated tokens in an output o o .
For a question q q , consider a budgeted decoding scheme that enforces an explicit effective token budget n n , e.g., truncating at n n tokens, or conditioning decoding on a target budget [ 12 ; 1 ] ,
and let Pr ⁡ ( correct ∣ q , n ) \Pr(\text{correct}\mid q,n) denote the resulting success probability.
We measure compute by an increasing cost function C ⁡ ( n ) C(n) , which is typically proportional to token count, and define the utility of allocating n n tokens to q q :
 U q ​ ( n ) ≜ Pr ⁡ ( correct ∣ q , n ) − λ ​ C ​ ( n ) , U_{q}(n)\ \triangleq\ \Pr(\text{correct}\mid q,n)\ -\ \lambda\,C(n), 
 (1) 
 where λ ≥ 0 \lambda\geq 0 controls the cost–accuracy trade-off.
Empirically, Pr ⁡ ( correct ∣ q , n ) \Pr(\text{correct}\mid q,n) often shows fast saturation on easier questions, whereas additional tokens can still yield substantial gains on harder ones, as shown in Figure 1(b) .
 Difficulty and practical proxies.
 Let d q d_{q} denote the difficulty of question q q under the current policy, where a larger d q d_{q} indicates a harder instance.
Since d q d_{q} is not directly observable, previous work often relies on human priors or dataset-specific heuristics [ 29 ; 17 ] .
Importantly, d q d_{q} is policy-dependent: as the policy improves, the same instance may become “easier”.
In the following analysis, we treat d q d_{q} as an abstract variable and only assume access to a difficulty score that ranks questions consistently with d q d_{q} , while we defer the concrete construction of such a proxy to Section 3 .
 Marginal optimality and effective token prices.
 To characterize when extra compute is worthwhile, we study how utility changes with the budget.
Although n n is a discrete integer token count, we quantify the benefit of adding a few more tokens by treating n n as a smooth budget variable and using the slope of Pr ⁡ ( correct ∣ q , n ) \Pr(\text{correct}\mid q,n) with respect to n n , which we approximate by a small difference between nearby budgets.
We then define the marginal gain as
 g q ​ ( n ) ≜ ∂ ∂ n ​ Pr ⁡ ( correct ∣ q , n ) g_{q}(n)\triangleq\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\Pr(\text{correct}\mid q,n) .
Differentiating Eq. ( 1 ) yields the marginal optimality condition:
 g q ​ ( n ) ≈ λ ​ C ′ ​ ( n ) , g_{q}(n)\ \approx\ \lambda\,C^{\prime}(n), 
 (2) 
 which balances marginal correctness gain against marginal compute cost.
Equivalently, a small budget increase d ​ n \mathrm{d}n changes the expected utility by
 ( g q ​ ( n ) − λ ​ C ′ ​ ( n ) ) ​ d ​ n (g_{q}(n)-\lambda C^{\prime}(n))\,\mathrm{d}n , so it is beneficial to increase n n while g q ​ ( n ) > λ ​ C ′ ​ ( n ) g_{q}(n)>\lambda C^{\prime}(n) and to stop once g q ​ ( n ) ≤ λ ​ C ′ ​ ( n ) g_{q}(n)\leq\lambda C^{\prime}(n) .
In practice, the marginal gain g q ​ ( n ) g_{q}(n) decays much faster on easy questions, but can remain sizable over a longer range on hard ones.
This view suggests interpreting λ \lambda as a dynamic effective token price : a larger λ \lambda makes tokens more expensive and induces earlier stopping, while a smaller λ \lambda encourages spending more compute when additional reasoning is productive.
 Difficulty-aware compute allocation.
 We now connect this optimality view to question difficulty.
Assume a mild structural property: for any fixed budget n n , harder questions admit no smaller marginal gains from extra compute, i.e., g q ​ ( n ) g_{q}(n) is non-decreasing in difficulty d q d_{q} . 1 1 
 1 
 This assumption is not required by our algorithm;
it serves as an explanatory lens that motivates difficulty-aware cost weighting. 
Under typical costs ( C ′ ​ ( n ) > 0 C^{\prime}(n)>0 and non-decreasing) and diminishing returns in n n (so g q ​ ( n ) g_{q}(n) eventually decreases with n n ), the balance point g q ​ ( n ) = λ ​ C ′ ​ ( n ) g_{q}(n)=\lambda C^{\prime}(n) occurs at a larger budget for questions with larger g q ​ ( ⋅ ) g_{q}(\cdot) .
In other words, harder questions naturally justify spending more tokens before marginal gains fall below marginal cost, whereas easy tasks saturate much earlier.
 It is therefore convenient to re-parameterize this per-question optimal budget as a question-dependent token price .
Specifically, we replace the global λ \lambda with a multiplier λ q \lambda_{q} and consider
 Pr ⁡ ( correct ∣ q , n ) − λ q ​ C ​ ( n ) \Pr(\text{correct}\mid q,n)-\lambda_{q}C(n) .
A (local) optimum n q ⋆ n_{q}^{\star} satisfies the condition g q ​ ( n q ⋆ ) = λ q ​ C ′ ​ ( n q ⋆ ) g_{q}(n_{q}^{\star})=\lambda_{q}C^{\prime}(n_{q}^{\star}) :
increasing λ q \lambda_{q} shifts the marginal-cost curve upward and yields a smaller n q ⋆ n_{q}^{\star} (earlier stopping), whereas decreasing λ q \lambda_{q} yields a larger n q ⋆ n_{q}^{\star} (more compute).
Thus, allocating more tokens to harder questions can be equivalently viewed as assigning them a lower effective token price, with smaller λ q \lambda_{q} .
In contrast, easy questions face a relatively higher price, with larger λ q \lambda_{q} .
 Finally, Section 3 turns this token-pricing view into a practical training objective by introducing a policy-internal proxy for relative difficulty and using it to modulate the effective compute cost across questions, enabling difficulty-aware compute allocation without external difficulty annotations.
 3 Method
 Following the optimality principles in Section 2 , we propose Coda , which builds on group-based RL and introduces a lightweight difficulty signal from rollouts for difficulty-aware compute allocation.
Concretely, Coda 
 1) estimates instance difficulty using the group success rate s q s_{q} ;
 2) converts s q s_{q} into an easy-side penalty weight w q easy w_{q}^{\text{easy}} and a hard-side bonus weight w q hard w_{q}^{\text{hard}} ; and
 3) shapes the base reward r i base r_{i}^{\text{base}} with a difficulty-gated length term that discourages redundant verbosity on easy instances while encouraging additional productive reasoning on hard ones.
 Preliminaries: group-based RL.
 In group-based RL methods such as GRPO [ 27 ] , the model samples a group of G G outputs O = { o 1 , o 2 , … , o G } O=\{o_{1},o_{2},\dots,o_{G}\} for each question q q .
Each output o i o_{i} then receives a binary base reward
 r i base = [ y ^ i = y i ] , r_{i}^{\text{base}}=\mathbbm{1}\!\left[\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}\right], 
where y ^ i \hat{y}_{i} is the final answer extracted from o i o_{i} and y i y_{i} is the ground truth.
The policy is then optimized with a policy gradient objective using token-level advantages computed from the group rewards.
Although this paradigm enhances reasoning with test-time scaling [ 40 ; 13 ] , it often incurs inference overhead due to verbose generations, especially on easy instances [ 30 ; 5 ] .
A naive remedy is to penalize length directly in the reward.
However, coarse length penalties can be brittle and frequently reduce token usage at the expense of accuracy [ 3 ; 1 ] .
 Group success rate as a difficulty proxy.
 Since instance difficulty is policy-dependent and not directly observed, we use a lightweight proxy derived from group-based rollouts.
Concretely, we compute the group success rate:
 s q ≜ 1 G ​ ∑ j = 1 G r j base , s_{q}\triangleq\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}r_{j}^{\text{base}}, 
 (3) 
 which serves as an internal difficulty signal: larger s q s_{q} indicates that q q is easier for the current policy, while smaller s q s_{q} indicates a harder question.
Importantly, s q s_{q} requires no external difficulty annotations and naturally evolves as the policy improves during training [ 29 ; 17 ] .
 Mapping difficulty to gating weights.
 Based on Eq. ( 2 ), we implement adaptive token pricing by scaling a length-dependent shaping term with two non-negative gates, w q easy w_{q}^{\text{easy}} and w q hard w_{q}^{\text{hard}} .
Specifically, w q easy w_{q}^{\text{easy}} increases the penalty on lengthy outputs when s q s_{q} is high (easy regime), while w q hard w_{q}^{\text{hard}} encourages deep reasoning when s q s_{q} is low (hard regime).
To instantiate this, we map s q s_{q} to the two gates as:
 w q easy = [ s q − τ easy 1 − τ easy ] + , w q hard = [ τ hard − s q τ hard ] + , w_{q}^{\text{easy}}=\Big[\frac{s_{q}-\tau_{\text{easy}}}{1-\tau_{\text{easy}}}\Big]_{+},\hskip 17.00024ptw_{q}^{\text{hard}}=\Big[\frac{\tau_{\text{hard}}-s_{q}}{\tau_{\text{hard}}}\Big]_{+}, 
 (4) 
 where [ ⋅ ] + = max ⁡ ( ⋅ , 0 ) [\cdot]_{+}=\max(\cdot,0) .
This construction ensures w q easy w_{q}^{\text{easy}} increases with s q s_{q} for easy instances, while w q hard w_{q}^{\text{hard}} increases when s q s_{q} is small for hard instances.
These thresholds satisfy τ easy > τ hard \tau_{\text{easy}}>\tau_{\text{hard}} and define the easy and hard regimes by determining when each gate becomes active.
 Shaping reward for adaptive compute.
 Let | o i | |o_{i}| denote the number of generated tokens in rollout o i o_{i} . To make the length term scale-invariant across prompts and training stages, we use a group-normalized length | o i | ~ \tilde{|o_{i}|} .
In our implementation, we normalize within the group using the group mean and standard deviation with a small ϵ \epsilon , and then apply a sigmoid σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) to map the length signal into (0,1).
We shape the base reward with a difficulty-gated length factor:
 r i = r i base ​ ( 1 + ( β ​ w q hard − α ​ w q easy ) ⏟ difficulty gate ⋅ σ ⁡ ( | o i | ~ ) ⏟ relative length ) , r_{i}=r_{i}^{\text{base}}\Bigg(1+\underbrace{\big(\beta\,w_{q}^{\text{hard}}-\alpha\,w_{q}^{\text{easy}}\big)}_{\text{difficulty gate}}\cdot\underbrace{\sigma(\tilde{|o_{i}|})}_{\text{relative length}}\Bigg), 
 (5) 
 where α ≥ 0 \alpha\geq 0 controls the easy-side length penalty and β ≥ 0 \beta\geq 0 controls the hard-side length bonus.
This form yields two desired effects:
 1) Trim redundancy on easy instances. For easy questions with large s q s_{q} , w q easy w_{q}^{\text{easy}} becomes positive and the term − α ​ w q easy ​ σ ​ ( | o i | ~ ) -\alpha\,w_{q}^{\text{easy}}\,\sigma(\tilde{|o_{i}|}) down-weights unnecessarily lengthy rollouts, encouraging the policy to stop earlier once reasoning becomes unproductive.
 2) Encourage deeper reasoning on hard instances without degenerate length seeking. For hard questions with small s q s_{q} , w q hard w_{q}^{\text{hard}} activates and the term + β ​ w q hard ​ σ ​ ( | o i | ~ ) +\beta\,w_{q}^{\text{hard}}\,\sigma(\tilde{|o_{i}|}) increases the reward for more deliberative rollouts.
Crucially, because the entire factor multiplies r i base r_{i}^{\text{base}} , incorrect rollouts ( r i base = 0 r_{i}^{\text{base}}=0 ) receive zero reward regardless of length; thus extra length is incentivized only when it is aligned with correctness.
 After computing shaped rewards { r i } i = 1 G \{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G} , we form group-relative advantages for policy optimization by subtracting the group mean reward: A ^ i , k = r i − mean ⁡ ( { r i } i = 1 G ) \hat{A}_{i,k}=r_{i}-\mathrm{mean}(\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}) , and apply the standard token-level policy gradient updates.

 4 Experiment
 4.1 Experimental Setup
 Models and Training Datasets.
 To assess the effectiveness of Coda across different model sizes, we select Qwen3-4B/8B/14B-Base [ 38 ] as backbone models.
We use base backbones, rather than instruction-tuned variants, to isolate the effect of post-training and to more clearly characterize the training dynamics induced by RL from a common pre-training initialization.
We conduct training on DeepScaleR [ 19 ] , a mathematics dataset of 40K problems spanning a wide range of difficulty levels, drawn from AIME (1983–2023), AMC, Omni-Math [ 7 ] , and STILL [ 23 ] .
 Baselines.
 We evaluate Coda against the following baselines:
 1) GRPO [ 27 ] , a widely used group-based RL method for improving reasoning performance, often benefiting from Long CoT and test-time scaling.
 2) Vanilla Length Penalty ( Vlp ) [ 3 ] , which introduces length penalties during RL training to avoid overly long generations.
 3) Adaptive Self-Recovery Reasoning ( Asrr ) [ 43 ] , an adaptive length-penalty approach for efficient reasoning that modulates the penalty strength based on estimated difficulty, imposing larger penalties on easier questions.
While effective at reducing token usage, these length-penalty baselines may trade accuracy for brevity by discouraging long reasoning even when it is beneficial on hard instances.
Additional details are provided in Appendix B .
 Implementation Details.
 We implement all RL methods using the open-source training framework verl [ 28 ] .
Unless otherwise specified, we train with a batch size of 128 128 and sample a group of G = 16 G=16 rollouts per prompt, with a maximum generation length of 16 ​ K 16K tokens per rollout.
We run training for one epoch, corresponding to 314 314 global update steps, and set the default sampling temperature to 1.0 1.0 .
All experiments are conducted on 8 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs.
For fair comparison, we keep the same training and sampling configuration for all baselines.
To trim redundancy on simple tasks while preserving deliberation for complex ones, we set α = β = 0.2 \alpha=\beta=0.2 , τ easy = 0.75 \tau_{\text{easy}}=0.75 , and τ hard = 0.25 \tau_{\text{hard}}=0.25 as stable defaults after light validation.
 Evaluation.
 To evaluate reasoning performance under both in-domain and out-of-domain distributions, we consider a diverse suite of general and mathematical benchmarks.
For general reasoning, we use CSQA [ 31 ] and GPQA [ 26 ] , which test commonsense and knowledge-intensive question answering, respectively.
For mathematical reasoning, we evaluate on SVAMP [ 25 ] , GSM8K [ 6 ] , MATH [ 11 ] , AMC23 [ 2 ] , AIME24 [ 21 ] , and AIME25 [ 22 ] , ranging from elementary to competition-level difficulty.
We evaluate model performance using accuracy and response length to assess both correctness and generation behavior.
To reduce single-sample decoding variance, we report mean ​ @ ​ 8 \mathrm{mean@8} by sampling 8 8 outputs per prompt. 2 2 
 2 
 For the 4B base model, weaker instruction following may inflate token cost through repetitive outputs. 
During inference, we use temperature 0.6 0.6 and top-p 0.95 0.95 .

In contrast, Coda dynamically conditions length shaping on estimated difficulty signals, yielding selective efficiency gains on easy problems while preserving sufficient compute to maintain strong performance on hard ones.

 6 Related Work
 Test-time scaling in LLMs.
 Recent advances in LLMs highlight the power of test-time scaling: spending more inference compute, often via longer deliberation traces, substantially improves reasoning performance [ 16 ; 24 ; 14 ; 8 ] .
In particular, Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has attracted growing attention for advancing reasoning capability, contributing to the emergence of large reasoning models (LRMs) [ 9 ; 15 ] .
With simple rule-based rewards, RLVR algorithms such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [ 27 ] can elicit Long Chain-of-Thought (Long CoT) [ 40 ; 13 ] . This supports deeper reasoning patterns such as searching and self-verification by allocating more inference-time compute [ 4 ; 42 ] .
However, this paradigm also incurs substantial overhead from extended outputs, amplifying the “overthinking” phenomenon: on easy queries, models often produce redundant restatement and backtracking with little accuracy gain [ 34 ; 30 ; 5 ] .
This leads to systematic inefficiency and motivates token-efficient compute allocation.
 Length control and efficient reasoning.
 A natural approach to efficiency is to control generation length explicitly.
Some methods require or allow users to specify an estimated target budget to guide inference-time compute [ 12 ; 1 ; 10 ] .
While this offers direct control, it can be sensitive in practice: underestimating the budget can sharply hurt performance, whereas overestimating the difficulty can waste tokens when extra reasoning is unnecessary [ 35 ] .
Another line of work reduces token usage by introducing length penalties or constraints during training [ 32 ; 36 ; 37 ; 3 ] , but such techniques can carry the risk of performance degradation, particularly on challenging instances that genuinely benefit from deep reasoning.
Relatedly, many methods described as adaptive reasoning aim to improve the accuracy–efficiency trade-off by learning when to think more or less, yet they frequently achieve efficiency mainly by accepting modest performance drops to unlock large cost savings over the query distribution [ 18 ; 33 ; 41 ; 35 ] .
This may reflect the difficulty of learning a universally optimal trigger, or RL optimization converging to a balance that prioritizes cost savings on typical inputs [ 17 ] .
Overall, these observations motivate difficulty-aware objectives that reallocate compute by trimming redundant verbosity on easy instances while preserving sufficient depth on hard ones, without relying on user-provided budgets or sacrificing correctness as the primary optimization target.
 7 Conclusion
 We study adaptive compute allocation for LLMs through an optimality lens, treating generation length as a controllable inference resource.
This motivates a difficulty-dependent allocation principle: penalizing verbosity on simple instances while encouraging deliberative rollouts on challenging ones.
Building on this insight, we introduce Coda , which leverages difficulty signals from group-based rollouts to dynamically modulate reasoning length through a dual-gated reward mechanism.
Across model scales and benchmarks, Coda produces genuinely adaptive behavior without external difficulty annotations or user-provided budgets, trimming overthinking on easy inputs—reducing token costs by over 60 % 60\% —while preserving effective Long CoT on hard ones.
Further analyses show that Coda remains stable under difficulty shifts and that both gating mechanisms are essential for maintaining effectiveness, yielding consistent efficiency gains without sacrificing accuracy on challenging tasks.

 Appendix B Details of Length-Penalty Baselines
 Vlp [ Arora and Zanette, 2025 ] .
 Vlp introduces a uniform length penalty to discourage overly long generations.
Specifically, the base reward r i base r_{i}^{\text{base}} is multiplicatively modulated by a length-dependent factor:
 r i = r i base ​ ( 1 − γ ⋅ σ ⁡ ( | o i | ~ ) ) , r_{i}=r_{i}^{\text{base}}\Bigg(1-\gamma\cdot\sigma(\tilde{|o_{i}|})\Bigg), 
 (7) 
 where σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) denotes the sigmoid function and | o i | ~ \tilde{|o_{i}|} represents the normalized output length.
The coefficient γ \gamma controls the overall penalty strength.
Unlike adaptive methods, Vlp applies a uniform penalty regardless of question difficulty.
It does not differentiate between easy and hard problems, and therefore may under-penalize easy samples or over-penalize difficult ones.
Following the original implementation and recommended hyperparameter settings, we set γ = 0.1 \gamma=0.1 .

 Asrr introduces an adaptive length penalty.
Rather than applying a uniform regularization, the penalty strength is dynamically modulated by the group-level success rate s q s_{q} :
 r i = r i base − ζ ​ [ s q − τ + ϵ ] + 1 − τ + ϵ ⋅ clip ⁡ ( | o i | − | o correct_shortest | L window ,  0 ,  1 ) . r_{i}=r_{i}^{\text{base}}-\zeta\frac{\left[s_{q}-\tau+\epsilon\right]_{+}}{1-\tau+\epsilon}\cdot\mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{|o_{i}|-|o_{\text{correct\_shortest}}|}{L_{\text{window}}},\,0,\,1\right). 
 (8) 
 The penalty is activated only when the estimated difficulty exceeds threshold τ \tau .
The clipped length term penalizes outputs that exceed the shortest correct solution | o correct_shortest | |o_{\text{correct\_shortest}}| within a local window L window L_{\text{window}} .
The penalty becomes stronger as the group-level success rate increases, thereby discouraging long reasoning on easier questions.
Although Asrr adaptively scales the penalty, it focuses on length suppression rather than compute reallocation across difficulty levels, potentially trading accuracy for shorter generations.

 Appendix C Details of L1
 L1 [ Aggarwal and Welleck, 2025 ] trains a model to follow a user-specified token budget by conditioning the prompt on a target length n gold n_{\text{gold}} .
Concretely, each training prompt is augmented with the instruction “Think for n gold n_{\text{gold}} tokens.”
During training, n gold n_{\text{gold}} is sampled uniformly from 𝒰 ⁡ ( n min , n max ) \mathcal{U}(n_{\text{min}},n_{\text{max}}) .
As a result, at inference time, L1 requires users to explicitly provide a token budget in the instruction, and the model then attempts to match the target length.
The reward is defined as:
 r i = r i base − η ⋅ | n gold − | o i | | . r_{i}=r_{i}^{\text{base}}-\eta\cdot\bigl|n_{\text{gold}}-|o_{i}|\bigr|. 
 (9) 
 Here η \eta controls the trade-offs between correctness and budget adherence.
Following the original implementation, we set η = 0.0003 \eta=0.0003 and use n min = 128 n_{\text{min}}=128 and n max = 10 ​ K n_{\text{max}}=10K , respectively.

 Tables 4 , 5 , and 6 provide a more detailed view of the design choices in difficulty-gated reward shaping.
Consistent with our main claims, we observe three key patterns.
 1) Varying the easy-side penalty strength α \alpha reveals a consistent pattern: moderate values substantially reduce token usage while preserving performance, whereas overly large α \alpha over-penalizes long responses and degrades accuracy.
This supports the view that the penalty should act as a selective regularizer rather than a global truncation mechanism.
 2) Performance is relatively insensitive to the exact value of β \beta within a reasonable range, but becomes unstable when correctness-gating is removed.
Notably, when β = 0 \beta=0 , the formulation reduces to a pure length-penalty scheme that trades accuracy for shorter responses, discouraging long outputs without encouraging productive reasoning.
This confirms that the gains of Coda arise from correctness-aligned incentives.
 3) The gating thresholds ( τ easy , τ hard ) (\tau_{\text{easy}},\tau_{\text{hard}}) show that extreme settings lead to suboptimal compute allocation, indicating that effective adaptiveness requires a balanced separation between easy and hard regimes.
Overall, these results further support that Coda achieves adaptive compute allocation by selectively modulating reasoning depth based on difficulty, rather than relying on uniform length control.
 Appendix F Analysis of Overthinking
 We follow prior studies on the overthinking phenomenon [ Sui et al., 2025 , Chen et al., 2025b ] :
on relatively simple instances, a model allocates excessive reasoning compute, which often manifests as redundant intermediate steps or repeated verification and backtracking loops, while yielding little or no improvement in correctness.
To further analyze this phenomenon, we compare responses from the GRPO and Coda 8B models on representative easy instances from SVAMP.
As illustrated below, GRPO often generates overly lengthy, step-by-step narratives. It may repeatedly restate the problem or elaborate on irrelevant details, even when the required operation is trivial.
In contrast, Coda typically produces a concise, task-sufficient reasoning trace that directly targets the required computation.
 Question 
 Danny collects bottle caps and wrappers. He found 65 wrappers and 5 bottle caps at the park. Now he has 31 bottle caps and 9 wrappers in his collection. How many more wrappers than bottle caps did danny find at the park?

 Appendix G Details of Reflective Words
 Following the methodology described in Section 5.4 , we use a curated set of 17 17 reflective words and phrases to identify instances of backtracking, self-correction, and verification in the model’s reasoning traces.
A response is classified as reflective if it contains at least one of the following terms:
 • 
 Prefix-based terms: 
 re-check , re-evaluate , re-examine , re-think , recheck , reevaluate , reexamine , reevaluation , rethink .
 • 
 Verification and retry phrases: 
 verify , check again , think again , try again , double-check , double check .
 • 
 Transition markers: 
 wait , yet .
 By monitoring these markers, we can distinguish between productive and redundant reasoning, where the latter generates lengthy responses without productive reflection.
 Appendix H Limitations and Future Work
 Improving difficulty estimation.
 Like other group-based RL methods such as GRPO, Coda relies on a group of G G rollouts per prompt to estimate advantages and derive a difficulty signal.
While this proxy is lightweight in practice and adapts naturally during training, it can be noisy when the rollout group size G G is small or when reward signals are sparse in certain domains.
As a result, developing more robust difficulty estimators (e.g., uncertainty-aware signals) could further improve compute allocation for adaptive reasoning.
 Scaling to longer contexts and larger models.
 Due to resource constraints, we do not systematically scale Coda to larger backbones or substantially longer context lengths.
For example, in the extreme Hard -training setting in Section 5.2 , we observed that a 16 ​ K 16K maximum response length resulted in a clipping ratio exceeding 5 % 5\% for extremely difficult instances, compared to less than 2 % 2\% in our main experiments.
This higher clipping ratio suggests that some productive reasoning trajectories can be prematurely truncated, potentially introducing additional optimization noise.
Consequently, the current context-length budget and training-set composition may not fully unlock the model’s potential under more generous compute budgets.
However, despite these constraints, systematic experiments across multiple benchmarks and model scales (4B/8B/14B) show that Coda consistently achieves genuine adaptiveness: Coda reallocates compute to trim overthinking on simple queries while preserving the complex, reflective reasoning patterns required for harder problems.
We leave systematic scaling studies to longer contexts and larger backbones for future work.
