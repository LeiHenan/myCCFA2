 Abstract 
 
 We present PEEK, a lightweight scheduling and eviction framework for both online (streaming) and offline (batch) LLM serving; this paper focuses on the online regime. PEEK maintains an incremental radix tree over the pending queue , exposing prefix-sharing clusters no existing engine surfaces. A low-overhead dual-walk matches the tree against the engine’s prefix cache to yield longest-prefix-match for every waiting request; PEEK then admits cluster pioneers first so siblings inherit the freshly cached prefix, a co-designed eviction hook protects blocks ancestral to queued demand, and a multi-lane stride scheduler bounds starvation. On SGLang and vLLM across five workloads up to 4 × \times H100 (DP=2 over TP=2), PEEK delivers up to 3.0 × / 2.6 × \mathbf{3.0\times/2.6\times} cache hit, 7.9 × / 7.1 × \mathbf{7.9\times/7.1\times} TTFT, 6.7 × / 5.5 × \mathbf{6.7\times/5.5\times} E2E, and 3.6 × / 4.5 × \mathbf{3.6\times/4.5\times} throughput gains over each engine’s strongest stock baseline (SGLang/vLLM), while matching baselines within noise on workloads with no exploitable prefix structure. Wins hold as KV-cache pressure and inference parallelism scale. 
 
 
 
 1 Introduction 

 
 Modern LLM serving engines optimize KV cache reuse: SGLang’s RadixAttention  ( Zheng et al., 2024a ) provides longest-prefix matching over cached blocks; vLLM’s PagedAttention  ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) enables block-level sharing via OS-style paging. Yet both stop at the cache—neither reasons about which waiting requests share prefixes with each other, leaving the predictive signal in the request queue unexploited (Table  1 ). SGLang’s prefix-aware policies ( LPM , DFS_WEIGHT ) remain bound to current cache state and degrade once the cache is cold; vLLM defaults to FCFS, which under memory pressure devolves into cache thrashing as interleaved prefix groups evict one another (§ 2 ). In both engines, scheduling and eviction are decoupled, precluding joint optimization—inefficiencies magnified in long-context workloads such as RAG and agentic pipelines. 
 
 
 We present PEEK , a lightweight scheduling and eviction framework for both online (streaming) and offline (batch) LLM serving; this paper focuses on online . Our motivation is a simple observation: the waiting queue is itself a structured workload whose prefix-sharing relationships can directly drive KV-cache reuse . PEEK retains LPM’s prefix-match objective but feeds it richer signals at lower overhead from an incremental radix tree via three mechanisms (Appendix Table  3 ):

 Substantial wins where prefix structure exists (§ 4.1 –§ 4.3 ).  PEEK also supports the offline (batch) regime, resting on the same observation: the pending queue makes future cache demand predictive , and the prefix relationships among waiting requests are the highest-signal structure to schedule and evict against. In contrast, PEEK-offline builds a one-shot prefix trie over the full submitted batch, DFS-traverses it to place sharing requests adjacently from a cold cache, and refines wave-level ordering against a shadow LRU model with the same queue-aware eviction hooks (Appendix  A.3.1 ). PEEK-offline delivers up to 1.6 × \times /1.8 × \times throughput on single-GPU (Qwen2.5-14B/1 × \times H100) and 2.4 × \times /8.0 × \times on multi-GPU (Qwen2.5-72B/2 × \times H100) over the strongest baselines (SGLang/vLLM), with near-zero overhead on non-sharing workloads (Appendix  A.3.2 ). 
 
 
 
 2 Background and Motivation 

 
 Prefix caching mechanisms. 

 
 We focus on SGLang and vLLM, the leading open-source LLM serving engines in production use; a request is a single completion call (prefill over the input prompt followed by autoregressive decoding). Both engines use PagedAttention to decouple logical token sequences from physical KV cache blocks. On top of this, vLLM employs APC (Automatic Prefix Caching), which hashes each block by token content and prefix position via chained hashing to identify reusable blocks. SGLang adopts RadixCache , organizing cached blocks in a radix tree for 𝒪 ⁡ ( D ) \mathcal{O}(D) per-request LPM. These mechanisms provide the architectural possibility for prefix sharing; actual performance depends on the scheduling and eviction policies discussed below. 
 
 
 
 Scheduling and eviction policies. 

 
 vLLM uses FCFS (arrival order, 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathcal{O}(1) , prefix-agnostic); SGLang offers LPM (Longest Prefix Match, 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ⋅ D ) \mathcal{O}(N\cdot D) per round) and DFS_WEIGHT , which groups requests by cache-tree position. All three bind to the current cache state and ignore inter-queue sharing: FCFS+APC barely helps under cache pressure (§ 4.1 ); LPM (greedy per-round) and DFS_WEIGHT (cache-tree-position grouping) both decide from current cache state and miss waiting-queue sharing. On the eviction side, both engines default to LRU , designed for OS page caches with unknown future access—but the LLM waiting queue encodes upcoming work, and LRU may evict prefixes that pending requests need next. SGLang’s LFU alternative is similarly queue-oblivious. Under heavy contention, all of these struggle (§ 4.1 ). 
 
 
 
 Scheduling-induced thrashing. 

 
 Stock LPM ranks by hit length h h and breaks ties by arrival—reactive, blind to future queue demand. Consider 8 cold chat requests interleaved across three tenants ( A 1 , B 1 , C 1 , A 2 , B 2 , C 2 , A 3 , B 3 A_{1},B_{1},C_{1},A_{2},B_{2},C_{2},A_{3},B_{3} ; cluster sizes 3/3/2; each tenant’s ∼ \sim 2K-token prompt uncached) on a KV cache that holds only one prompt at a time. All tied at h = 0 h{=}0 , LPM admits in arrival order: prefill A A , evict for B B , evict for C C , refill A A — 0% hit . PEEK reorders the queue by cluster via the pending tree to A 1 , A 2 , A 3 , B 1 , B 2 , B 3 , C 1 , C 2 A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},B_{1},B_{2},B_{3},C_{1},C_{2} : each prompt prefills once and is reused— 62.5% hit . At 100+ tenants and 2 2 – 16 × 16\times KV pressure, the gap widens: cache-state-bound schedulers cannot anticipate which prefix the queue will demand next (§ 4.1 ). 
 
 
 The gaps.   (i) Visibility: existing schedulers ignore inter-queue sharing. (ii) Coupling: scheduling and eviction are decoupled. (iii) Co-design: scheduling and eviction share the same queue-demand signal but no engine exploits it jointly. PEEK closes all three: an incremental radix tree over the waiting queue exposes prefix-sharing clusters the engines cannot see, driving cluster-aware admission and a co-designed eviction hook that protects blocks queued requests will reuse (§ 3 ). 
 
 
 
 
 3 PEEK: P r e dictiv e Queue-Informed K V Cache Management 

 
 PEEK rests on a simple observation: the waiting queue is itself a structured workload . To utilize its signals, PEEK introduces an incremental radix tree over the pending queue (§ 3.1 ) supporting three mechanisms (Figure  1 ): (1) a dual-walk (§ 3.1 ) yielding all-LPM via O ⁡ ( C × D ) O(C{\times}D) tree co-descent on SGLang’s radix cache and per-request hash probes on vLLM’s hash cache; (2) cluster-aware admission (§ 3.2 ) admits the pioneer first so siblings reuse the cached prefix, paired with an eviction hook that frees only blocks that no pending cluster needs; (3) a multi-lane stride scheduler (§ 3.2 ) interleaving cache-locality with fairness. 
 
 
 PEEK pairs a Rust core ( ∼ \sim 1.6 kLOC; pending tree, dual-walk, cluster/score lookups; PyO3) with ∼ \sim 800 LOC Python shims per engine that monkey-patch SGLang’s RadixCache / Scheduler and vLLM v1’s Scheduler / BlockPool ; cLPM sort runs in Python over Rust-computed signals. Environment flags toggle PEEK per-binary, no upstream fork (Appendix  A.1.2 ).

 3.1 Pending Radix Tree and Dual-Walk for LPM 

 
 The pending radix tree is a compressed trie over the waiting prompts. Each leaf carries the request IDs ( terminators ) whose prompt ends there; each internal node holds a pending_count —the number of waiting prompts passing through. Edges store multi-token runs via radix compression, so size scales with distinct prefix branches, not raw token count. PEEK maintains the tree incrementally: insert on arrival, remove on admission/completion, both O ⁡ ( D ) O(D) along the prompt’s path (typically O ⁡ ( log ⁡ N ) O(\log N) ). The tree is always current at scheduling time—no per-cycle rebuild. Two requests share a queued prefix iff their prompts pass through a common internal node with pending_count ≥ 2 \texttt{pending\_count}\geq 2 ; the deepest such node is the request’s cluster , and its pending_count the cluster size. 
 
 
 Each cycle, PEEK obtains the LPM hit h ⁡ ( r ) h(r) for every waiting request and separately reads its cluster signals (size, ancestor score) from the pending tree at O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) per request. On SGLang’s radix cache, PEEK dual-walks —tree-vs-tree co-descent of the pending tree against the radix cache (Algorithm  1 ; Figure  6 in Appendix  A.1.4 )—collapsing N N per-request lookups into C C per-cluster lookups, O ⁡ ( C × D ) O(C{\times}D) vs. O ⁡ ( N × D ) O(N{\times}D) , an amortization of N / C N/C that is large for high-sharing workloads where C ≪ N C\ll N . On vLLM’s flat block-hash cache, no structural co-descent is possible: PEEK calls the engine’s per-request find_longest_cache_hit once per request ( O ⁡ ( N × b ) O(N{\times}b) , b b = block-hash chain length), then reads cluster signals separately from the pending tree—an O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) traversal exposing the cluster structure stock vLLM cannot see.

 3.3 Guards 

 
 PEEK’s per-cycle cost (dual-walk + sort) would otherwise apply even when the queue has no sharing. The pending tree exposes a has_sharing primitive—an O ⁡ ( # ​ root-children ) O(\#\text{root-children}) scan returning true iff some root-child has pending_count ≥ 2 \texttt{pending\_count}\geq 2 —available for callers to short-circuit cLPM. In addition, on SGLang the stock scheduler’s queue-depth fallback (LPM degenerates to FCFS once the waiting queue exceeds 128 requests) provides a second cheap exit. Empirically these two paths together leave PEEK matching FCFS within ± \pm 1.5% on workloads with no exploitable structure (W4, W5; § 4.4 ); the dual-walk + sort cost is amortized to the noise floor in those regimes.

 DL shortens TTFT/E2E by 2.4–15.1% on SGLang A/C and vLLM C, recovering the singleton-starvation cost that GM imposes ; elsewhere it trades a small TTFT regression for queue-aging fairness.  Takeaway 1. 

 
 For high-sharing workloads, all cLPM-based PEEK policies substantially outperform stock baselines on both engines on cache hit, TTFT, E2E, and throughput, with the gain scaling as KV pressure rises. The biggest gain comes from cLPM’s cluster-aware scoring atop stock LPM. GM further pushes cache hit but at the cost of delaying singletons, causing a noticeable loss on TTFT and E2E. DL and PE recover those two ends, ultimately establishing cLPM+GM+DL+PE as the most performant policy on both engines.

 5 Related Work 

 
 Prefix-aware request scheduling. 
 Online: Preble  ( Srivatsa et al., 2025 ) co-optimizes KV reuse with distributed load balancing across replicas. Fu et al. (2024) reorder by predicted output length to reduce head-of-line blocking. DLPM  ( Cao et al., 2025 ) adds a delay-bound fairness contract on top of LPM. Dexter et al. (2025) prove the prefix-aware scheduling problem NP-hard and propose a k k -LPM approximation. HotPrefix  ( Li et al., 2025 ) drives admission and CPU/GPU promotion from a moving average of past hits ( cache hotness ). SOLA  ( Hong and others, 2025 ) arbitrates prefix grouping against per-request SLOs. Autellix  ( Luo and others, 2025 ) reorders at the program level for agent workloads, capturing inter-call dependencies. Offline / batch: BatchLLM  ( Zheng et al., 2024b ) groups requests by global prefix sharing for high-throughput batched inference. BlendServe  ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) reorders an offline workload to jointly maximize prefix/KV reuse and balance compute- vs. memory-bound phases, exploiting full visibility into the request set before submission. PEEK differs by forming groups from the pending queue itself via an incremental prefix trie—robust to cold, stale, or contended cache—and uses the same trie for eviction so the two co-optimize one signal; it is intra-replica and composes beneath cross-replica routers, fairness contracts, or SLO arbiters. PEEK-offline (Appendix  A.3 ) sits in the BatchLLM/BlendServe regime; this paper targets the online case with no full-workload visibility. 
 
 
 KV cache reuse across prompts. 
A separate axis supplies the capability to share KV across prompts: PromptCache  ( Gim et al., 2024 ) (module-level KV via positional re-encoding), RAGCache  ( Jin et al., 2024 ) (hierarchical RAG cache), ChunkAttention  ( Ye et al., 2024 ) (attention over a prefix tree), Hydragen  ( Juravsky et al., 2024 ) (fused shared-prefix kernel), CachedAttention  ( Gao et al., 2024 ) (session-scoped state), CacheBlend  ( Yao et al., 2024 ) (block reuse across retrieved chunks), CacheGen  ( Liu et al., 2024 ) (compressed KV streaming), LMCache  ( LMCache contributors, 2025 ) (distributed shared KV). These are mechanisms for how KV is stored; PEEK is the policy for which blocks are cached and in what order requests run, composing atop any of them. 
 
 
 Within-sequence KV compression and eviction. 
H2O  ( Zhang et al., 2023 ) keeps heavy-hitter tokens by attention history; SnapKV  ( Li et al., 2024 ) clusters and prunes tokens at prefill; Scissorhands  ( Liu et al., 2023 ) drops decayed-attention tokens; InfiniGen  ( Lee et al., 2024 ) offloads cold KV with prefetch; KIVI  ( Yuan et al., 2024 ) quantizes KV to 2-bit. These act per-request along the sequence dimension; PEEK acts across requests along the block-allocation dimension and is fully orthogonal. 
 
 
 Disaggregated and distributed serving. 
DistServe  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ) and Splitwise  ( Patel et al., 2024 ) disaggregate prefill from decode; Mooncake  ( Qin et al., 2025 ) extends this to a distributed KV pool; Llumnix  ( Sun et al., 2024 ) migrates running requests across instances. These reshape where requests run; PEEK reshapes when they run within a replica, and the two axes compose. 
 
 
 Cross-replica prefix-affinity routing. 
Gateways such as llm-d  ( Red Hat and IBM and Google contributors, 2025 ) score backends by prefix-affinity, queue depth, and KV utilization to route requests to the replica most likely to hit warm cache. This is cross-replica; PEEK improves intra-replica scheduling and sits beneath such a router. 
 
 
 Future-aware cache management. 
KVFlow  ( Pang et al., 2025 ) predicts future cache needs from workflow-graph structure; Marconi  ( Pan and others, 2025 ) forecasts reuse from prior traffic. PEEK shares the goal but derives demand from the live pending queue via direct reference counting—no execution graph, no learned forecast—and is complementary across within-tick (PEEK) and across-tick (KVFlow/Marconi) horizons. 
 
 
 
 6 Conclusion 

 
 PEEK treats the waiting queue as a first-class signal for KV cache management via an incremental radix tree, a dual-walk yielding all-LPM in O ⁡ ( C × D ) O(C{\times}D) , cluster-aware admission/eviction, and an adaptive fairness lane. Across five workloads on SGLang/vLLM up to 4 × \times H100, PEEK lifts cache hit 3.0 × / 2.6 × \mathbf{3.0\times/2.6\times} , TTFT 7.9 × / 7.1 × \mathbf{7.9\times/7.1\times} , E2E 6.7 × / 5.5 × \mathbf{6.7\times/5.5\times} , throughput 3.6 × / 4.5 × \mathbf{3.6\times/4.5\times} , matches baselines on no-sharing traffic, and composes beneath cross-replica routers and SLO arbiters atop any prefix-reuse mechanism. Limitations: the N / C N/C amortization shrinks toward 1 in singleton-dominated traffic (the has_sharing guard handles this regime); the cLPM sort still runs in Python; evaluation is on H100 only, leaving smaller-memory accelerators and multi-modal prefixes for future work. Code will be released.

 A.1.2 Implementation Details 

 
 Native core. 

 
 The pending radix tree, dual-walk, and per-rid cluster/score lookups are implemented in ∼ \sim 1.6 kLOC of Rust ( src/tree.rs , src/pending.rs ) and exposed to Python via PyO3 ( peek._core ). Nodes live in a contiguous arena indexed by NodeId , with FxHashMap children keyed by the first edge token, FxHashSet terminators, and per-node pending_count ; insert / remove run in O ⁡ ( D ) O(D) with no allocation in the hot path. The cLPM sort itself runs in Python ( O ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) O(N\log N) over the Rust-computed signals); pushing the tree, dual-walk, and per-rid score/cluster computations into Rust eliminates the per-compare Python callback overhead that dominates stock SGLang LPM at large N N . 
 
 
 
 SGLang integration ( ∼ \sim 814 LOC of Python). 

 
 peek.engines.sglang.patch_hook monkey-patches two surfaces at import time. RadixCache.__init__ is patched to install PeekDemandStrategy (§ 3.2 ) in place of the default heap-based eviction. Scheduler.process_input_requests is patched to diff-sync the pending tree against self.waiting_queue on every scheduling iteration—a mutation-path-agnostic sync that tolerates any downstream queue manipulation (append, pop, list-comprehension reassignment) without further hooks. The same hook overrides SchedulePolicy.calc_priority (the entry point that internally calls _sort_by_longest_prefix in stock SGLang) to invoke PEEK’s cLPM sort. 
 
 
 
 vLLM integration ( ∼ \sim 800 LOC of Python). 

 
 peek.engines.vllm.patch_hook hooks three v1 scheduler entry points: Scheduler.schedule (sync + reorder self.waiting ), Scheduler.add_request (sync-on-arrival fast path), and Scheduler.finish_requests (drop completed rids). Because vLLM’s prefix cache is hash-keyed rather than tree-structured, PEEK additionally maintains an inverted index block_hash → demand \text{block\_hash}\to\text{demand} alongside the pending tree, and patches BlockPool.get_new_blocks to scan the free-block queue (capped at 4 ​ N 4N entries) and prefer zero-demand victims over the default LRU pick. Recency/decay eviction modes also patch BlockPool.touch and cache_full_blocks to fabricate per-block timestamps that vLLM does not track natively. vLLM v1 spawns its EngineCore via multiprocessing.spawn , so monkey-patches in the parent do not inherit; PEEK ships a sitecustomize.py shim that re-imports the hook in every child interpreter. 
 
 
 
 Activation surface. 

 
 A single environment flag selects scope: PEEK_ONLINE_SCHEDULER for cluster-aware scheduling, PEEK_ONLINE_EVICTION for queue-aware eviction, PEEK_ONLINE_CLPM for the multi-lane scheduler, PEEK_ONLINE_EVICTION_MODE ∈ \in { plain , cluster , recency , decay } for the eviction variant, and PEEK_ONLINE_CLPM_BIGLANE_SHARE for the cache-locality lane share (default 0.7). With no flags set, both shims are no-ops and the engine runs unmodified. Because integration is by monkey-patching, no fork or PR against either upstream engine is required, and PEEK tracks SGLang’s main branch and vLLM 0.19.1 with byte-identical baselines. 
 
 
 
 
 A.1.3 Pending Radix Tree: Build and Clustering 

 
 The pending radix tree (§ 3.1 ) is a textbook compressed trie with two augmentations: per-node pending_count and per-leaf terminators . Insert descends from the root following matching edges, splitting any edge on token divergence (creating a new internal node) and adding a new leaf for the request, then bumps pending_count along the root-to-leaf path— O ⁡ ( D ) O(D) , where D D is the prompt’s tree depth. Remove reverses: walk to the request’s terminator, remove the rid, decrement pending_count along the path, and prune empty branches—also O ⁡ ( D ) O(D) . Cluster walks the request’s prompt down the tree and returns the deepest node v v with pending_count ​ ( v ) ≥ 2 \texttt{pending\_count}(v)\geq 2 ; cluster size is pending_count ​ ( v ) \texttt{pending\_count}(v) . The tree therefore costs O ⁡ ( N ⋅ D ) O(N\cdot D) per cycle in total, dominated in practice by the scheduler’s O ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) O(N\log N) sort (§ 3.2 ). 
 
 
 
 A.1.4 Dual-Walk for Longest Prefix Match

On SGLang’s radix cache, PEEK runs the recursive dual-walk: tree-vs-tree co-descent collapses N N per-request lookups into C C per-cluster lookups (Algorithm  1 ). The recursion descends a pending edge only when the edge matches in full—a partial match terminates the branch, since deeper nodes cannot extend the cached prefix. On vLLM’s flat block-hash cache, no structural co-descent is possible: PEEK calls the engine’s per-request find_longest_cache_hit once per waiting request to obtain h ⁡ ( r ) h(r) , then walks the pending tree separately to read cluster size and ancestor score—the LPM lookup is not amortized, but the cluster signal stock vLLM’s FCFS lacks is still surfaced.

 A.1.6 Eviction Motivation Example 

 
 Even with good scheduling, uninformed LRU eviction can undo prefix sharing. Consider three prefix groups A, B, C with a cache holding two prefixes. Wave 1 processes A then B, leaving both cached (B most recently used). Wave 2 needs A and C. When C arrives and the cache is full, LRU evicts A—the least recently accessed—but A is exactly what wave 2 needs next, forcing full recomputation. A queue-aware policy inspects pending requests, sees A is needed and B is not, and evicts B instead—preserving A for an immediate hit.

 (a) LRU evicts A (least recent), but A is needed next. 
 
 Wave 1 Wave 2 A B A C Cache: after wave 1 A B evict B after eviction A C A: hit A is preserved for immediate reuse 
 (b) Queue-aware: evicts B (not needed), preserves A. 
 
 Figure 7 : LRU vs. queue-aware eviction. After wave 1 caches A and B, wave 2 needs A and C. LRU (a) evicts A—the least recently used—forcing recomputation. Queue-aware eviction (b) inspects pending requests, preserves A, and evicts the unreferenced B.

 Appendix A.3 PEEK-offline 

 
 A.3.1 Mechanisms 

 
 In the offline (batch) regime the entire workload is known in advance and throughput is the sole objective; fairness and per-request latency tails are not first-order constraints. PEEK-offline exploits this by replacing the per-cycle pending tree of PEEK-online with a one-shot, full-batch construction, executed entirely as client-side preprocessing through the engine’s public API. 
 
 
 Phase 1: Prefix trie DFS reordering. 

 
 PEEK-offline builds a token-level prefix trie over all N N submitted requests by inserting the first D D tokens of each prompt (default D = 128 D{=}128 ); each node records the requests terminating there and the subtree request count. A depth-first traversal that visits children in descending subtree-count order emits a permutation π \pi in which prefix-sharing requests are adjacent, even when the engine cache is cold. Construction is O ⁡ ( N ⋅ D ) O(N\cdot D) and traversal is O ⁡ ( | T | ) O(|T|) with | T | ≪ N ⋅ D |T|\ll N\cdot D under sharing; at N = 5,000 N{=}5{,}000 the entire reorder completes in < < 50 ms on a single CPU core. 
 
 
 
 Phase 2: Shadow LRU cache and wave-aware refinement. 

 
 The Phase 1 ordering is partitioned into waves of size W W approximating the engine’s running batch. A client-side shadow LRU cache, hashed identically to vLLM/SGLang ( k i = hash ( k i − 1 , tuple ( s [ i B : ( i + 1 ) B ] ) ) k_{i}=\texttt{hash}(k_{i-1},\texttt{tuple}(s[iB{:}(i{+}1)B])) , B = 16 B{=}16 ), simulates per-block residency in O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) amortized time and is reconciled against the engine’s reported cached_tokens after every completion. A second cache-aware DFS within each wave scores children by 
 

 
 
 key ​ ( v ) = cache_frac ​ ( v ) ⋅ 10 4 − future_refs ​ ( v ) + count ​ ( v ) ⋅ 10 − 3 , \texttt{key}(v)=\texttt{cache\_frac}(v)\!\cdot\!10^{4}\;-\;\texttt{future\_refs}(v)\;+\;\texttt{count}(v)\!\cdot\!10^{-3}, 
 
 
 strictly preferring already-cached groups (exploit), deferring groups demanded by later waves (preserve), and using subtree count only as a tiebreaker. A queue-aware eviction hook scans the remaining batch to derive per-block reference counts, evicting unreferenced blocks first and protecting referenced blocks via log-dampened depth-weighted priority to avoid ossification. 
 
 
 
 Guard conditions. 

 
 Three cheap pre-checks skip all PEEK work when reordering cannot pay for itself: duplication ( > > 50% exact-duplicate sequences), coverage ( < < 10% of requests share ≥ \geq 32 tokens), and depth (average sharing depth < < 64 tokens). When any guard fires, PEEK-offline reduces to FCFS at O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) overhead. 
 
 
 
 
 A.3.2 Experiment Results 

 
 PEEK-offline is evaluated on two configurations: Single-GPU (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, 1 × \times H100 80 GB, FP16) and Multi-GPU (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, 2 × \times H100, TP=2, FP8), both on SGLang v0.5.9 and vLLM v0.10.x at production-default memory (SGLang mem_fraction_static =0.80, vLLM gpu_memory_utilization =0.90). Single-GPU uses six workloads (three high-sharing: shared system prompts on LooGLE, mixed traffic, multi-turn ShareGPT chat; three low-sharing: few-shot MMLU, code completion on HumanEval/MBPP, single-turn diverse). Multi-GPU uses six production scenarios (Legal RAG, Long-Doc Summarization, Multi-Agent, Enterprise Search, Mixed Traffic, plus a non-sharing control). Baselines are FCFS, LPM, and DFS-Weight with LRU/LFU on SGLang; FCFS and APC with LRU on vLLM. 
 
 
 Single-GPU. 

 
 On the three high-sharing workloads, PEEK-offline beats the strongest baseline by 1.1–1.6 × \times on SGLang (cache hits 72–84%) and 1.0–1.8 × \times on vLLM (71–85%). The largest gains are on shared system prompts where 100 prefix groups arrive randomly interleaved (FCFS achieves only 1% hits); the smallest are on multi-turn chat, where sequential conversation turns supply natural adjacency (FCFS already at 50% hits). Prefill reduction accounts for 89–97% of total latency improvement; the remainder is decode-side, an indirect benefit of freeing GPU compute sooner. On the three low-sharing workloads, all policies are within 3% of each other—the guards correctly skip reordering. P99/Mean latency ratios stay within 0.05 of the best baseline across all six workloads, confirming no starvation. 
 
 
 
 Multi-GPU. 

 
 Scaling to Qwen2.5-72B on 2 × \times H100, PEEK-offline beats the strongest baseline by 1.6–2.4 × \times on SGLang (cache hits 90–96%) across five high-sharing scenarios and by 1.6–8.0 × \times on vLLM (90–98%) across four scenarios. The largest gain is on Multi-Agent (16K shared prefixes), the smallest on Mixed Traffic (moderate natural adjacency). Non-sharing controls remain within 5% of baseline. 
 
 
 
 Component ablation and scaling. 

 
 A reorder-only variant (DFS without shadow cache or eviction) matches the full system on every workload, while eviction-only delivers no benefit in isolation—DFS reordering supplies the temporal locality that queue-aware eviction then preserves. Sweeps confirm gains scale monotonically with cache pressure: prefix length 512 → \to 4096 grows the gain from 1.0 × \times to 3.0–3.8 × \times ; group count 20 → \to 200 from ∼ \sim 1 × \times to 2.2 × \times ; concurrency 16 → \to 256 stays in the 1.3–1.8 × \times range. Under variable prefix lengths ( σ \sigma =0.0–0.6) PEEK’s hit rate holds at 83–85% while baselines degrade. 
 
 
 
 Takeaway. 

 
 Across both single- and multi-GPU offline serving, queue-derived prefix structure is the dominant lever for cache efficiency; the same insight that drives PEEK-online in this paper carries over to the batch regime, where the per-cycle pending tree collapses to a one-shot batch trie but the underlying co-design of prefix-grouped admission and queue-aware eviction is unchanged.
